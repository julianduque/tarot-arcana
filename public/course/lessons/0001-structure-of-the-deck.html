The Fourfold Key: How the 78 Cards Are Built

Lesson 1 of 22 · Foundations · Reading ~5 min · Practice separately

Identify the families in your deck, make an observation before an interpretation, and distinguish the cards’ history from the Hermetic framework you will study.

Begin with the object in your hands

Lay out the four Aces. Before naming an element, describe what you see: a hand, a vessel, a garden, a crown, a branch. “The cup overflows” is an observation. “An emotion is becoming difficult to contain” is an interpretation. “Cups correspond to Water in the Golden Dawn system” is a traditional attribution. You will use all three, but they answer different questions. Keeping them separate gives your reading an argument you can examine.

The standard structure of the decks studied here is 22 trumps and 56 suit cards. Each suit contains Ace through Ten and four courts: fourteen cards, repeated four times. Aces count among the forty pips in this course. Two through Ten make thirty-six, a distinction that matters when we reach the decans. A court belongs to the Minor Arcana even when it portrays a crowned person. “Major” is not a ranking of how important a real-life concern ought to be.

A historical beginning and a later esoteric interpretation

Surviving Italian cards and records place tarot in fifteenth-century gaming culture. The Hebrew letters and Golden Dawn correspondences taught here are later occult applications. The relationship can be meaningful without requiring a story in which the first makers secretly designed the entire modern system. The number twenty-two becomes useful to a later interpreter; numerical correspondence alone does not prove common origin.

Our principal working framework is Golden Dawn-derived Hermetic Qabalah. It gives you a consistent vocabulary for comparing cards, and it influenced the worlds in which Waite, Smith, Crowley and Harris worked. It is not the only legitimate tarot vocabulary. Keep the historical question “who first used this?” distinct from the interpretive question “what does this help me understand?”

The four suits as fields of experience

In this framework, Wands correspond to Fire: initiative, appetite, will and expenditure of energy. Cups correspond to Water: receptivity, attachment, imagination and relationship. Swords correspond to Air: distinction, speech, judgement and conflict. Pentacles correspond to Earth: resources, labour, embodiment and consequences. These are overlapping fields, not four sealed departments of life. A difficult conversation can involve all four.

Imagine proposing a community event. Wands asks what gives you the energy to begin; Cups, what relationships make the work meaningful; Swords, what decisions and agreements are needed; Pentacles, what time, equipment and money the proposal requires. This is an exercise in elemental thinking. It does not assign a fixed diagnosis to a person because one suit appears in a draw.

Study the Aces as distinct proposals. The Ace of Wands offers a living branch, but it does not show a completed project. The Ace of Cups offers an overflowing vessel, but it does not identify a particular relationship. The Ace of Swords presents an upright blade and crown, but clarity can be used harshly. The Ace of Pentacles shows a tangible object and cultivated ground, but an opportunity still requires work. Each root contains possibilities; its context determines which matter.

Three decks, with their differences visible

RWS means Rider–Waite–Smith: Waite’s published commentary and Smith’s images are related sources, not interchangeable ones. “Waite says” requires a textual statement. A plausible reading of a painted detail should be introduced as an interpretation unless an artist’s account establishes it.

Thoth refers here to Crowley’s commentary and Harris’s paintings. Its Earth suit is Disks; its courts are Knight, Queen, Prince and Princess. The course’s functional comparison pairs Thoth Knight with RWS King and Thoth Prince with RWS Knight. It does not mean those pictures or personalities are identical. Thoth also has its own trump names and doctrinal emphases, which will be visibly marked throughout the studies.

Deviant Moon is Valenza’s distinct imaginative world. Shared suits and ranks make comparison possible, but his images deserve their own observation and commentary. Do not assume the RWS Strength/Justice numbering transfers: consult the numerals on your actual cards. The publisher’s booklet identifies Justice as VIII. Course diagrams labelled Golden Dawn are study overlays, not claims about a hidden system Valenza necessarily intended.

A first disciplined reading

Choose one card and write three short lines: “I see…”, “In this tradition…”, and “For this question, I might read…”. If you cannot yet supply the traditional meaning, leave that line open and consult the reference. An honest gap is more useful than an invented explanation. At this stage, successfully recognizing a card’s family and giving a careful visual description is enough.

Begin a practice record below. Record uncertainties as well as discoveries. When a later lesson changes your understanding, keep the earlier attempt and explain the revision. That record will show the difference between recognition, recall and interpretation far better than a congratulatory sentence at the end of a page.

Check your reasoning

These recognition checks support practice. They do not certify interpretive skill.

Which count excludes the Aces?

Each suit has nine numbered pips after the Ace: 9 × 4 = 36.

“A hand emerges from a cloud” is primarily what?

Begin with the image before assigning a meaning to its features.

Which statement correctly labels the framework?

This course chooses a convention and names it rather than universalizing it.

Retrieve before revealing

Where does “Cups = Water” belong: observation, attribution or application?

Attribution: it identifies a relationship in the selected tradition. An overflowing cup is an observation; reading it as an invitation to accept support is an application.

What does matching structure across two decks establish?

It establishes comparable card families. It does not establish identical imagery, authorial intention, historical origin or interpretation.

Practice and spaced review

  1. Sort a physical deck into trumps and four suits. Count 22, 40 pips, and 16 courts; explain why Two–Ten alone totals 36.
  2. Describe one Ace from RWS and its counterpart from each of your other decks. Record one visible difference before consulting a guide.
  3. Write the three-line reading method for a card you do not know. Return to it after lesson 4.

At your next session, recall one distinction from this lesson before opening it. Revisit it about a week later and again when a subsequent card study uses it. Mark uncertain material for review rather than treating a lookup as failure.

Read with the sources

Historical orientation: The Met: Before Fortune-Telling — History and Structure of Tarot Cards. Read the discussion of surviving fifteenth-century packs. Distinguish the history of the cards from later esoteric applications.

Primary text: Waite, The Pictorial Key, Part I: The Four Suits. Read the deck divisions alongside your physical cards. Note which claims are description and which interpretation.

Creator and publisher: Patrick Valenza, Deviant Moon Tarot companion. Use your deck’s booklet or the matching companion entry. The publisher describes the book; it does not provide every card’s full explanation. Where you lack the text, mark your own visual interpretation as such.
