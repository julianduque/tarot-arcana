Reading for Others: Presence, Ethics & the Conversation

By the end of this lesson You'll be able to run a reading for another person as a respectful, two-way conversation — framing it, holding hard cards with care, keeping their agency intact, and staying inside ethical bounds. This is the skill your mission points at most directly.

Reading for someone is a different act

Reading for yourself is reflection. Reading for someone else adds a person who is often hopeful, anxious, or vulnerable — and who may take your words to heart more than you expect. The cards are the easy part now; the relationship is the skill. A good reading is a conversation you host, not a verdict you pronounce.1

The shape of a session

  1. Consent & frame. Confirm they actually want a reading. Set expectations: this explores possibilities and choices, it doesn't predict fixed fate. Agree roughly what you'll and won't cover.
  2. Find the real question. Help them move from "will he come back?" to "what's going on in this relationship and what can I do?" The better question makes the better reading.
  3. Lay the spread (three-card or Celtic Cross) and read it aloud from structure, thinking out loud so they can follow.
  4. Make it two-way. Ask what they see, whether a card lands, who a court might be. Their recognition is data — and it keeps them the author of their own story.
  5. Close with a takeaway. End on something actionable and hopeful: one thing to notice or do. Never leave someone alone with a frightening image.

Holding the hard cards

Death, the Tower, the Ten of Swords, the Devil — these will come up, and how you handle them is the ethics of reading. Frame them toward movement and choice:

  • Death → an ending making room for something new, not literal death.
  • The Tower → a sudden clearing of what was unstable, not doom.
  • The Devil → what binds them (habit, fear) — and that chains can be removed.

Read the difficulty honestly, but always with a door left open. Fear disempowers; your job is the opposite.

The ethical spine (recap & deepen from Lesson 6). Preserve agency (tendencies, not fate). Empower, don't frighten. Stay in your lane — refer medical, legal, and financial questions to real professionals; never diagnose or predict death. Get consent, and protect privacy — don't read on absent third parties. And know your limits: it's fine to say "I won't read on that."

Things that quietly go wrong

  • Fortune-telling drift. The querent pushes for certainty ("just tell me yes or no"). Hold the frame — offer insight and options, not guarantees.
  • Over-reading the reader. Your own hopes or judgements about their life leak in. Name the card's structure first to stay honest.
  • Cold-reading applause. Vague, flattering statements that could fit anyone feel impressive and teach you nothing. Be specific and falsifiable instead.
  • Dependency. Someone wanting the cards to decide for them. Keep handing agency back: "what would you want to do here?"

Practice — read for a real person

Homework

  1. Read for a willing friend. Get consent, help them shape an open question, lay a three-card or Celtic Cross, and read it as a conversation — asking what they see at each step.
  2. Close well. End with one actionable, hopeful takeaway. Afterwards, ask them: did it feel useful and respectful? Note their answer.
  3. Practise a hard card. Rehearse aloud how you'd deliver Death, the Tower, and the Devil to a nervous querent — honestly, but leaving a door open.
  4. Wisdom — test it in the world. Post a reading you did (cards + your interpretation) to r/tarot, or read Mary K. Greer on reading for others, and compare approaches.

Why debrief with the querent: their honest feedback is the tightest feedback loop you have for the one skill books can't teach — presence.
