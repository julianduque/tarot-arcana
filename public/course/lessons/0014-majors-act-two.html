Majors Act II: The Inward Turn (8–14)

By the end of this lesson You'll read the middle seven trumps — Strength through Temperance — each from its full correspondence stack, and feel how this act moves the journey beneath the surface: courage, solitude, fate, surrender, and death.

Act II: the descent into the unconscious

Having built a self in the daylight, the Fool now turns inward. These seven trumps are the work of the unconscious — meeting the inner beast, seeking in solitude, submitting to the turning of fate, letting go, and being remade. Act II holds the deck's great cards of surrender: the Hanged Man and Death.

Reading all seven from structure

Same derivation as Act I — letter + astrology + path — for all seven, in order:

VIII · Strength — Teth (serpent, primal life-force) + Leo (fixed fire, the lion) + the path joining Mercy (Chesed) and Severity (Geburah). So: not brute force but the gentle mastery that tames the beast — courage that works with the animal power, balancing mercy and severity.

IX · The Hermit — Yod (hand; the smallest letter, the divine spark) + Virgo (discerning, solitary earth) + the path from Chesed to Tiphareth. So: withdrawal and inner search — the lamp he holds is the Yod-spark, wisdom carried into solitude to light the way back.

X · Wheel of Fortune — Kaph (the palm, a hand that opens and closes) + Jupiter (expansion, luck) + the path from Chesed to Netzach. So: cycles and fortune, the turning of fate — what the grasping-then-opening hand gives and takes away.

XI · Justice — Lamed (ox-goad, which directs and corrects; the tallest letter) + Libra (the scales, cardinal air of balance) + the path from Geburah to Tiphareth. So: truth, balance, and cause-and-effect — the goad that corrects each act toward its exact consequence.

XII · The Hanged Man — Mem, a Mother letter, element Water, on the path from Geburah to Hod. Pure water is stillness, depth, reflection; hanging inverts the ordinary view. So: willing surrender and suspension, the wisdom that comes only when you stop struggling and let yourself be turned upside-down.

XIII · Death — Nun (fish, the life moving under dark water) + Scorpio (fixed water, the sign of death and regeneration) + the path from Tiphareth to Netzach. So: not literal death but the deep transformation that clears the old to release the new — Scorpio's whole business.

XIV · Temperance — Samekh (a prop or tent-peg, that which supports) + Sagittarius (the archer aiming high, mutable fire) + the path from Tiphareth to Yesod, up the central pillar. So: the alchemical blending of opposites into something that holds — moderation, patience, the art of the right mixture aimed at a higher mark.

See these paths in place on the lettered Tree in Lesson 8 — Act II is paths 19–25, clustered around Tiphareth, the balanced heart of the Tree.

Numbering reminder. Waite's RWS puts Strength = VIII and Justice = XI (older decks reverse the numbers). The letters and signs above are the Golden Dawn standard and don't move with the numeral — Strength is always Leo, Justice always Libra.

Retrieval practice

From memory: give the astrological attribution for Strength, the Hermit, and Temperance.

Strength = Leo; the Hermit = Virgo; Temperance = Sagittarius — three signs of the fire/earth/fire sequence here.

Which two Act II trumps are the deck's great "surrender" cards, and why?

The Hanged Man (Mem/Water — willing suspension) and Death (Scorpio — transformation through release). Both require letting go rather than forcing.

Practice before the next lesson

Homework

  1. Lay out Act II (Strength → Temperance) and recite each card's letter, astrology, and one-line meaning; check the table.
  2. Derive two from letter-image + astrological force — try Death and the Hanged Man, the two you're most likely to face in real readings.
  3. Trace the paths. Mark paths 19–25 on your Tree drawing; note that this act clusters around Tiphareth, the balanced heart of the Tree.
  4. Read the primary source: Waite's entries for these seven; notice how much gentler his "Death" and "Hanged Man" are than pop culture suggests.

Why focus on Death and the Hanged Man: they frighten querents most, so deriving their real (transformational) meaning protects your future readings from fatalism.
