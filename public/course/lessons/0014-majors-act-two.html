Majors Act II: The Inward Turn (8–14)

Lesson 14 of 22 · Major Arcana · Reading ~25 min · Practice separately

Study Strength through Temperance as distinct responses to force, judgement, suspension and transformation, including Thoth’s Lust, Adjustment and Art.

This chapter includes full card studies. Read the core first, then study a few cards per sitting. RWS artwork, Golden Dawn correspondences and Thoth comparisons are explicitly labelled.

An inward turn requires distinctions

The middle seven cards are often described as a movement into the unconscious. That narrative can support reflection, but the images make more particular demands. Strength’s relation to an animal, Hermit’s raised lamp, Wheel’s changing arrangement, Justice’s scales, Hanged Man’s suspension, Death’s procession and Temperance’s pouring cannot be replaced by a single instruction to “let go.”

Work with two or three cards per sitting. Recall a card from lesson 13 before beginning each new group. The aim is to move from recognizing a title to explaining a difference with evidence from the image and the selected tradition.

Explore the correspondences

Golden Dawn framework. RWS and Thoth names are shown separately. The diagram’s convention is always labelled.

Strength and direction are not interchangeable

Compare Strength with Chariot. How is force engaged in each image? One comparison is between responsive handling and directed control, but test that idea against the picture. Neither card simply tells a querent to become stronger. In a difficult conversation, maintaining a direction and responding to resistance are different tasks. A reading becomes useful when it makes that difference visible.

Suspension, ending and withdrawal

Hermit, Hanged Man and Death can all appear in a reading about stepping away. Ask what distinguishes deliberate inquiry, a suspended position and an ending that changes what can continue. Recommending passive endurance in every such case would ignore the differences. Recommending immediate transformation would do the same. Look at what the figures can do, how the scene is organized, and what the position asks.

Alchemy: a relation to study, not a synonym for improvement

Alchemy enters this course through specific images and texts, especially the Thoth Lovers and Art. In that comparative study, separation and combination, differentiated partners and a changed compound, become ways to examine the relation between the two cards. “Alchemy” should not be added to any vaguely transformative experience simply to give it esoteric weight.

When studying Temperance and Art, ask what is being joined, whether the constituents remain unchanged, and how the operation is represented. The symbolic problem is more exact than “find a happy medium.” A mixture can require a new form or a difficult reconciliation. Do not force a universal sequence of named alchemical stages onto all twenty-two trumps unless a particular source actually proposes it.

Observe the RWS image

A woman in a white dress bends toward a lion, with one hand near its upper jaw and the other beneath its mouth. Flowers crown her head and form a garland around her waist. Above her is the same sideways figure-eight seen over the Magician. The lion's tongue is visible and its tail curves upward; the woman does not carry a whip or weapon. A green landscape and a distant mountain lie beneath a yellow sky. Observe the contact before calling it taming: the picture shows closeness and controlled pressure, while the exact degree of resistance is open to interpretation. Its force comes from a relationship between two living figures rather than from armor, chains or a throne.

RWS interpretation and application

Waite's Part II places this strength in a religious account of higher nature and the passions; Part III also includes courage. A contemporary interpretation can address patience, emotional regulation, generosity under pressure and the ability to remain present with something powerful. It can also expose coercion dressed as kindness, endurance that has become self-neglect, or suppression that leaves the underlying impulse unchanged. The card does not require a person to stay in a harmful situation. In an ordinary conflict, ask how to protect a boundary while reducing unnecessary escalation. Courage may consist in naming anger accurately, asking for support or allowing a feeling without letting it dictate every action.

Golden Dawn framework and interpretive use

Teth and Leo belong to path 19, joining Chesed and Geburah in the Golden Dawn Tree. The conventional serpent association of Teth and the lion of Leo offer images of concentrated vitality. The bridge between Mercy and Severity gives a contemplative frame for power exercised with proportion. RWS numbers this card VIII; Thoth's corresponding Teth–Leo trump is XI, Lust. The correspondence is useful for comparison, but it does not mean the art and doctrine are unchanged. Treat the path as a prompt to examine the relation of force and care, not as a formula that settles a conflict without knowing its circumstances.

Thoth — Lust

Crowley makes ecstatic participation central to Lust, represented by Babalon riding the Beast. The raised cup and the transformed animal-human relationship make this much more than a renaming of Smith's Strength. Where the RWS scene invites attention to close, measured contact, Thoth dramatizes an expansive embrace of vital energy. That comparison is an interpretation of the designs, not a claim that Waite simply hated desire or Crowley recommended every impulse. For practice, distinguish feeling fully alive from treating intensity as authority. Enthusiasm, sexuality and creative appetite can be meaningful without becoming self-justifying. Lust asks what wholehearted participation might mean; the surrounding question must still establish consent, consequences and the kind of participation actually available.

Distinguish a similar card

Strength differs from the Nine of Wands even when both suggest endurance. The Nine of Wands foregrounds a guarded, wounded person holding a defensive position; Strength foregrounds the manner of relationship with active force. Someone can keep going while becoming increasingly armored against everything. Ask whether the useful work is maintaining a necessary defense, changing the quality of engagement, or recognizing that persistence has reached its limit.

Try a focused reading exercise

Recall a tense exchange. Describe the impulse you felt, the action you chose and its effect. Write one response that preserves the energy of the feeling while using less coercion.

Observe the RWS image

An elderly, bearded figure stands alone on a pale mountain height, wrapped in a long gray cloak. One hand raises a lantern containing a six-pointed star; the other holds a staff that reaches the ground. The face turns downward toward the light and the space immediately ahead. There are no companions, buildings or trees in the foreground, and the background is largely empty. The lantern is small compared with the surrounding space, yet it is held visibly outside the cloak. Notice the difference between being alone and hiding: the figure's beacon can be seen. The sparse composition asks the viewer to attend to a limited light, a stable footing and a deliberately narrowed field of concern.

RWS interpretation and application

Waite's Part II emphasizes attained insight offered as a beacon, rather than a person merely searching for truth; Part III includes prudence. A contemporary reading can still explore study, solitude, mentoring and the need to withdraw from noise. Label that as an application rather than presenting it as Waite's entire intention. The difficult range includes isolation, inaccessible expertise and endless preparation that avoids participation. Ask what the period of withdrawal is for and what will indicate that it has done its work. A useful Hermit process often returns something to the world: a clearer judgment, a taught skill or a more honest understanding of a limit. Solitude becomes purposeful when its relation to others remains thinkable.

Golden Dawn framework and interpretive use

Yod, Virgo and path 20 link Chesed to Tiphareth in the Golden Dawn diagram. Yod's conventional hand association can remind you that discrimination must become a practice; Virgo supplies a symbolic language of careful sorting and refinement. The path can be contemplated as generosity or accumulated experience finding an integrated center. This is an interpretive use of the map, not evidence that solitary people are inherently wiser. The lamp offers a better test: what does your understanding illuminate, and where does it stop? Precision includes the ability to say what has not yet been learned.

Thoth — The Hermit

Harris surrounds the Hermit with wheat, a serpent-encircled egg and Cerberus, while the lamp contains a solar center. Crowley connects Yod and Virgo with concealed fertility and Mercury's role as guide through the underworld. This is considerably more than an endorsement of solitude. Smith emphasizes a visible beacon on a height; Thoth places the light within processes of gestation and passage. As a reading inference, an insight may need a protected interval before it can become useful work. Ask whether the idea is still developing, ready for a test, or being sheltered from scrutiny. The sexual and generative symbolism belongs to Crowley's system and need not become a claim about a reader's gender, reproductive life or personal destiny.

Distinguish a similar card

The Hermit and the Four of Swords both suggest stepping back, but the Four of Swords more directly offers a scene of repose and recovery. The Hermit carries an active light and staff. If someone is exhausted, prescribing deeper introspection may add labor when rest is needed. If someone is rested but confused, a structured period of study may help. Identify the purpose of withdrawal before praising it.

Try a focused reading exercise

Spend fifteen quiet minutes investigating one narrowly defined question. Record the clearest thing learned, one unresolved point, and the person or action that could test your understanding.

Observe the RWS image

A large orange wheel floats among clouds, with letters around its rim and smaller symbols inside. A sword-bearing sphinx sits at the top; a descending serpent follows one side, while a red animal-headed figure rises along the other. At the four corners are winged figures with books: a human, an eagle, a bull and a lion. None is an ordinary person turning the wheel by hand. Compare the central circular movement with the steadier corner arrangement. Some forms seem to rise, some descend, and the sphinx occupies a precarious balance above. The design provides several positions within a cycle rather than a single winner, loser or fixed destination.

RWS interpretation and application

Waite's Part II presents changing life within a providential order; Part III favors fortune and elevation. A contemporary reading can examine a turning point, a recurring pattern, an opportunity dependent on timing or conditions outside personal control. It can also expose fatalism or the habit of attributing every success to skill and every failure to luck. Ask which part of the situation repeats, which part has changed, and what remains actionable. The card does not promise an improvement merely because it appears upright. A cyclical model is useful only if it helps you notice conditions and prepare a response, rather than assuming the next phase must resemble an imagined cosmic schedule.

Golden Dawn framework and interpretive use

Kaph, Jupiter and path 21 join Chesed to Netzach in the Golden Dawn arrangement. Kaph's conventional palm association provides a mnemonic for receiving or holding; Jupiter adds expansion and the broadening of possibilities. The path can be used to contemplate how an established order encounters desire and changing circumstances. These associations do not establish that Jupiter caused an event or that a hand contains a readable destiny. Let the symbolic expansion generate practical questions: what opportunity is becoming available, what grows beyond its proper scale, and what would remain sound if the favorable conditions changed?

Thoth — Fortune

Harris's ten-spoked wheel is surrounded by lightning and carries the Sphinx, Hermanubis and Typhon. Crowley associates their changing positions with the three gunas and alchemical principles, and treats Jupiter as the incalculable factor rather than guaranteed good luck. Smith's book-bearing corners give the changing wheel a steadier frame. As a contemporary inference, distinguish an external opening from the capacity to respond to it. A meeting, technology or deadline can alter available options without selecting the best one for you. The comparison supports examining both turbulence and continuity. Do not turn Crowley's cross-cultural synthesis into a universal explanation of Hindu philosophy or a prediction that one phase must arrive on schedule.

Distinguish a similar card

The Wheel and the Tower both unsettle control, but the Wheel emphasizes changing position within a larger cycle. The Tower shows an abrupt breach of an existing structure. A seasonal slowdown may call for adapting to a cycle; discovering that the business model is unsound calls for confronting a structural problem. Do not use a comforting cycle story to minimize a rupture that requires repair.

Try a focused reading exercise

Draw a simple timeline of a recurring situation. Mark what repeats and what differs this time. List one action under your control and one condition you can only monitor.

Observe the RWS image

A crowned figure sits facing the viewer between two pillars, with a purple hanging behind the seat. One hand holds an upright sword; the other suspends a pair of scales. The robe is red, with a green mantle or collar, and a pale shoe appears below the hem. The sword makes a strong vertical line, while the scale beam adds a horizontal comparison. The gaze is direct and the composition almost symmetrical, but the two instruments have different jobs. One measures; the other can enact a decision. Before reading the card as punishment, notice that no accused person, sentence or injured party appears. The scene presents the apparatus and posture of judgment rather than its completed outcome.

RWS interpretation and application

Waite's Part II distinguishes this moral ordering from the Priestess's spiritual mystery; Part III includes equity and bias. A modern reading can address evidence, proportion, agreements, accountability and the correction of an imbalance. Its difficult range includes selective standards, excessive severity or rules applied without relevant context. The card is not a guarantee that a court will decide fairly, and its appearance cannot establish guilt. In everyday use, ask what information is missing, which standard is being applied and whether the same standard would be accepted if the positions were reversed. An accountable decision explains both its reasons and its consequences. Justice can require revision as much as enforcement.

Golden Dawn framework and interpretive use

Lamed, Libra and path 22 connect Geburah with Tiphareth in the Golden Dawn Tree. Lamed's conventional ox-goad association offers a mnemonic for direction and correction, while Libra contributes the image of weighing. The path can be contemplated as force brought into proportion around a center. RWS numbers Justice XI; the Thoth counterpart is VIII, Adjustment, while Lamed and Libra remain the comparison points. A symbolic balance is not a claim that the world automatically compensates every injury. Use the correspondence to examine a proposed correction and its effects, especially whether the attempt to restore fairness creates another avoidable imbalance.

Thoth — Adjustment

Harris's masked dancer balances on her toes beneath the ostrich feathers of Maat; sword and scales organize an exact equilibrium. Crowley distinguishes this cosmic exactness from human ethical ideas about justice and connects the figure with the Fool. Smith's seated judge offers a more immediately institutional image. As a practical inference, RWS can focus on applying an explicit standard, while Thoth asks how changing one part affects an entire arrangement. Equal treatment and equal outcomes may diverge, and a well-intended correction can have uneven consequences. This does not make ethics irrelevant. It adds the discipline of observing effects. Treat Adjustment as a shift of image and doctrine, not merely a new title for an unchanged court judgment.

Distinguish a similar card

Justice and Judgement share the language of reckoning, but Justice weighs an act or arrangement against a standard. Judgement presents a summons into a changed condition. Reviewing whether an agreement was honored is a Justice question; deciding what your life now asks after that review may be a Judgement question. Keeping them distinct prevents every moment of accountability from becoming a dramatic declaration of destiny.

Try a focused reading exercise

Write a disputed claim, the evidence for it, the evidence against it and the standard you would use to decide. Identify one piece of new information that could change your judgment.

Observe the RWS image

A person hangs upside down by one ankle from a wooden structure shaped like a T. The free leg crosses behind the suspended leg, while the arms are held behind the body. A bright halo surrounds the head, and the face appears calm rather than contorted with pain. Leaves grow from the wood, keeping the support visibly alive. The blue tunic and red leggings create a clear division within the inverted body. No crowd, executioner or visible struggle appears. Describe the actual suspension before deciding whether it is chosen. The image establishes an unusual position and a pause in ordinary movement, while leaving the circumstances of that pause largely outside the frame.

RWS interpretation and application

Waite's Part II stresses living suspension and concealed spiritual significance; Part III includes sacrifice and discernment. A contemporary reading may explore waiting, changing perspective, releasing an ineffective tactic or accepting that a process cannot be forced. The difficult range includes paralysis, costly self-erasure and assigning virtue to suffering merely because it is endured. Do not assume the person has freely chosen their constraint. Ask what is actually suspended, what remains possible and whether a different view changes the available action. A useful pause has a relationship to learning or reality; an indefinite pause can become a way to avoid a decision. The card invites examination of that difference rather than automatic praise of surrender.

Golden Dawn framework and interpretive use

Mem, Water and path 23 link Geburah to Hod in the Golden Dawn diagram. Mem is one of the mother letters. Water offers a symbolic language of suspension, receptivity and altered orientation, while the path can be contemplated as force meeting the structures of thought. This does not mean water is naturally passive or that a card proves sacrifice is necessary. As a reading lens, ask what happens when the usual method of exerting force is withdrawn. A pattern that looked fixed may become visible as a chosen assumption, but some constraints remain material and require help rather than a change of attitude.

Thoth — The Hanged Man

Harris suspends the inverted figure from an ankh, places green disks at bodily extremities and introduces a serpent below. Crowley interprets the scene through baptism and the sacrificial formula of the Aeon of Osiris, whose continuing authority he then challenges. Smith's living tree and calm face make suspension and possible insight especially accessible; Thoth adds a pointed religious argument about sacrifice. As a contemporary inference, ask whether an account of necessary suffering still serves a valid purpose. Care and commitment can have real costs, but those costs deserve conscious examination. The comparison neither condemns every sacrifice nor praises endurance automatically. A stalled process should be assessed before it is called an initiation.

Distinguish a similar card

The Hanged Man and Death both interrupt ordinary continuity, but suspension leaves a situation held between possibilities. Death emphasizes an ending or transformation that changes what can continue. Waiting for a delayed answer is not the same as receiving a final refusal. Identify whether the matter is genuinely unresolved or whether calling it a pause is protecting you from acknowledging an ending.

Try a focused reading exercise

Name one stalled situation. Separate the constraint you cannot alter, the tactic you can stop, and the information the pause might reveal. Set a concrete condition for reviewing the pause.

Observe the RWS image

An armored skeleton rides a white horse through a scene containing people of different ages and social positions. The rider carries a black banner with a pale five-petaled flower rather than a visible weapon. A crowned figure lies on the ground; a religious figure stands with hands joined; a young person kneels and another figure turns away. The horse's forward motion crosses the entire group. In the distance, a sun shines between two towers, and a river with a small vessel runs through the landscape. The image includes endings and ongoing movement at once. Avoid saying everyone is already dead: the people respond differently, and the picture preserves those different reactions to the same approaching force.

RWS interpretation and application

Waite's Part II discusses transformation alongside mortality; Part III includes endings and inertia. It is inaccurate to claim that Waite meant only a cheerful change or never considered literal death. In present-day reflective practice, however, a drawn card is not evidence that someone will die. Use the image to investigate what has ended, what is changing beyond easy reversal, and what attachment makes recognition difficult. The difficult range includes clinging to a finished role or discarding something prematurely in the name of transformation. Renewal is a possibility to examine, not a benefit owed immediately after loss. Let grief, relief and uncertainty remain distinct rather than requiring every ending to produce instant growth.

Golden Dawn framework and interpretive use

Nun, Scorpio and path 24 join Tiphareth to Netzach in the Golden Dawn Tree. Nun's conventional fish association and Scorpio's water symbolism offer a mnemonic for life moving through processes that are difficult to observe directly. The path can support contemplation of identity, desire and the ways an attachment changes its form. This is an interpretive system, not a biological mechanism or a forecast of mortality. Ask what persists through the change and what cannot be carried forward unchanged. The attribution becomes useful when it sharpens attention to an actual transition rather than turning every uncomfortable event into a grand transformation.

Thoth — Death

Harris's skeleton wears an Osirian crown and sweeps a scythe through forms emerging in bubbles. Crowley's account connects the scorpion, serpent and eagle with different aspects of transformation and alchemical putrefaction. Smith gives a solemn procession encountered by people with different responses; Thoth gives the operation itself a dancing, generative form. As a practical inference, a transition reorganizes several parts of life at different speeds. A job ending may alter routines, status, income and relationships without producing one immediate rebirth. Attend to those material processes before announcing a new identity. The alchemical imagery supports a meditation on continuity through change, while allowing loss to remain painful and its eventual outcome uncertain.

Distinguish a similar card

Death and the Tower are often treated as interchangeable disaster cards. Death emphasizes an ending and the altered continuity around it; the Tower emphasizes a breach that exposes or destroys a structure. An expected retirement and a sudden institutional collapse can both transform life, but they require different responses. Ask whether the central task is acknowledging an ending, stabilizing after a shock, or attending to both.

Try a focused reading exercise

Choose a completed phase of your life. List what ended, what continued and what emerged only later. Then identify one current transition without assuming it must follow the same pattern.

Observe the RWS image

A winged figure stands with one foot on land and the other in shallow water, pouring a stream between two cups. The figure wears a white garment with a triangle set inside a square on the chest, and a small disk appears on the forehead. Irises grow beside the pool. A narrow path leads away toward distant hills and a bright crown-like light. The body remains centered while the hands manage a transfer across space. Notice that the scene is not motionless: water is moving, and the figure balances across different surfaces. Its calm arises within an ongoing operation, making the image more specific than a general instruction to avoid strong feelings.

RWS interpretation and application

Waite's Part II describes harmonizing psychic and material natures; Part III offers moderation and management. A contemporary reading can examine proportion, pacing, cooperation and the work of making different needs coexist. It may suggest changing a routine by small adjustments rather than demanding an immediate total solution. The difficult range includes bland compromise, excessive accommodation or insisting on moderation when a clear boundary is needed. Ask what is being mixed, in what proportion, and for what purpose. Successful integration preserves enough difference for the ingredients to contribute something. Temperance is therefore active work: observing an effect, adjusting the mixture and giving the result time to show whether it holds.

Golden Dawn framework and interpretive use

Samekh, Sagittarius and path 25 connect Yesod with Tiphareth along the central axis of the Golden Dawn Tree. Samekh's conventional support association offers a mnemonic for something that sustains a process, while Sagittarius supplies a directed aim. The path can be contemplated as bringing image, habit or aspiration toward an integrated purpose. This does not prove that every compromise is spiritually balanced. The useful interpretive question is whether a practice supports the intended direction over time. A small daily method can carry an ambitious aim more reliably than occasional enthusiasm, provided the aim and the method remain open to review.

Thoth — Art

Harris combines the Lovers' royal pair into a single figure working over a vessel; the lion and eagle exchange their earlier colors. Crowley calls Art the fulfillment of that marriage and relates it to Sagittarius opposite Gemini. The paired cards develop analysis and synthesis: distinguish the constituents, then transform their relation. As a contemporary inference, integration is not merely splitting a difference between competing demands. A new design or agreement may be needed. Compare two teams bargaining over hours with teams redesigning work so both constraints are met. RWS Temperance already contains active harmonization, so do not caricature it as timid restraint. Thoth intensifies a specific alchemical doctrine through substantial changes in the image.

Distinguish a similar card

Temperance differs from Justice because the primary question is how a mixture works, not whether an action satisfies an explicit standard. A fair allocation of resources can still be impractical when the parts cannot cooperate. A workable compromise can still be unfair. Read the two together by asking both whether the arrangement functions and whether the people affected have good reason to accept it.

Try a focused reading exercise

Choose two competing needs in a routine. Design one seven-day experiment that gives each a defined place. Specify what you will observe and one adjustment you can make after the trial.

Read Thoth’s changes explicitly

RWS VIII Strength is compared with Thoth XI Lust; RWS XI Justice with Thoth VIII Adjustment. RWS XIV Temperance is compared with Thoth XIV Art. Those three relationships are labelled in each study. Match name and image before working through a correspondence. Numeral alone will send you to the wrong comparison for VIII and XI.

Reflection without compulsory transformation

Choose a current situation in which something is paused, ending or being combined. Compare Hanged Man, Death and Temperance/Art as three different questions. The cards may help clarify an experience; they do not establish that a painful event was necessary or that it must make you wiser. An honest account can include loss that remains loss alongside any later possibility.

Check your reasoning

These recognition checks support practice. They do not certify interpretive skill.

Which pair has the same numeral in RWS and Thoth?

Temperance/Art are XIV. Strength/Lust and Justice/Adjustment exchange VIII/XI across the decks.

Which use of alchemy is most precise?

Work from particular images and texts rather than a decorative label.

What should distinguish Hanged Man from Death?

Compare processes, not only a shared theme of change.

Retrieve before revealing

Why is Art more than a renamed Temperance?

The Thoth study gives the card its own imagery and alchemical emphasis. A shared position in a deck structure does not make the paintings or doctrinal treatment identical.

How can “let go” be too vague for this group?

Suspension, withdrawal, ending and deliberate combination describe different processes. Identify which the image and context support before recommending an approach.

Practice and spaced review

  2. Put RWS Temperance beside Thoth Art. Identify what the image and primary commentary contribute separately.
  3. Revisit one Act I card in relation to an Act II card. Explain a tension rather than treating the pair as a fixed sequence.
