A Field Guide to Spreads (& Designing Your Own)

By the end of this lesson You'll have a working library of spreads — from one card to ten — each with its layout diagram and the job of every position, and you'll be able to design your own spread for any question. Match the spread to the question, or build the one it needs.

A spread is a set of questions, laid out in space

Every spread is just positions — each asking the cards one specific question — arranged so the layout itself carries meaning (a timeline reads left-to-right; a "core" card sits in the centre; "above/below" splits conscious from hidden). Learn a handful, and learn to build your own. Below, each spread shows its diagram; the number in each box is the order you lay the cards.

One card for a daily focus, a check-in, or a yes-and-how nudge. The fastest way to build card familiarity over time — pull one each morning and note how it played out.

7 · The Celtic Cross (ten cards)

The full, many-angled picture — taught in depth, with its diagram and reading order, in Lesson 10. Reach for it on big, tangled questions.

Bigger still: a year-ahead wheel lays 12 cards (one per month) in a circle around a 13th "theme of the year" card at the centre — a New Year ritual more than a question-spread. Lay the twelve clockwise from the top.

Designing your own — seven principles

  1. Start from the question. The spread serves the question, never the reverse. Get it open and clear first (Lesson 6).
  2. One job per position. Each asks exactly one thing. If a position needs an "and," split it in two.
  3. No overlap. Two positions asking nearly the same thing waste a card and blur the read.
  4. Cover the facets. Together the positions should map the question's real dimensions — inner/outer, cause/advice, self/other.
  5. Let layout carry meaning. Timeline across, core in the centre, above/below for conscious/hidden, two columns for two options.
  6. As small as the question needs. Three sharp positions beat ten vague ones. Add a card only when it earns its place.
  7. Include a "blind spot." A position like "what I'm not seeing" or "advice" often carries the reading's most useful card.
Worked example — build one live. Question, reframed open: "What do I need to understand about this job offer, and how should I approach the decision?" Four single-job, non-overlapping positions, laid as a diamond so the layout speaks — the offer on top, its hidden cost below, your real need and the wisest approach on either side:

Practice before the capstone

Homework

  1. Run three of the spreads above on real questions — say the single card, the five-card cross, and the horseshoe — laying them out exactly as diagrammed.
  2. Design a spread for a question none of them fit. Write the open question, then 3–5 single-job, non-overlapping positions, and sketch the layout so the positions carry meaning.
  3. Pressure-test it against the seven principles — does any position overlap? need splitting? Is a facet missing?
  4. Read the primary source: browse reader-designed spreads on r/tarot or Mary K. Greer's blog and critique their positions with your new eye.

Why sketch the layout: deciding where a position sits forces you to say what it relates to — half a spread's meaning lives in its geometry.
