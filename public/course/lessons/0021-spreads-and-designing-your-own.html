A Field Guide to Spreads (& Designing Your Own)

Lesson 21 of 22 · Reading Practice · Reading ~5 min · Practice separately

Choose positions that answer a specific question, recognize leading or overlapping positions, and define a spread before drawing.

A spread is a set of interpretive commitments

A position asks a question of a card. “My stance” and “a useful approach” are not the same question even if you might answer them with similar words. Defining positions first limits the temptation to reinterpret an awkward card by changing its role. The spread’s geometry can help you remember those relationships, but the written definitions do the explanatory work.

Use a small spread when the question is narrow. A single card can focus attention; a line can distinguish aspects; a cross can compare a situation with influences around it. More cards bring more relationships to interpret, not automatically more reliable information. Add a position only when it asks something the existing positions leave out.

A small working library

For one card, ask “What should I examine in how I approach this?” For three, use Situation → Approach → What to attend to. A temporal line can be used if you identify it as a reflective narrative rather than an established account of past or future facts. The Celtic Cross remains available for questions with genuinely distinct facets.

A five-card cross can use 1 situation, 2 challenge, 3 foundation, 4 available support, 5 a question to carry forward. Define “foundation” as the basis of the present situation, not a licence to infer secret childhood causes. Define support as something to investigate in known circumstances, not a promised rescue. Those limits make the positions more useful.

A seven-card horseshoe can separate context, present focus, overlooked perspective, difficulty, support, approach and a possible direction. Label the sequence before laying it out. “Overlooked perspective” invites reflection on your own attention; it does not guarantee that a hidden fact will be revealed.

Compare options without asking a card to decide

For two alternatives, compare the same questions on both sides: what attracts me to the option and what demands examination about it. Add a central position for the value or constraint that matters to the decision. A card in that centre does not choose a guaranteed winner; it helps you ask a clearer question about your priorities.

Avoid a biased layout such as “Why A is sensible” beside “What is wrong with B.” It builds the conclusion into the questions. Likewise, “the hidden cost” presupposes there is a concealed cost. A more open phrase is “what costs or obligations need examination.” The ability to revise position wording is part of reading skill.

Relationship readings without invented access to another mind

Use “my stance,” “what I can observe about our interaction,” and “a constructive conversation to consider” for an individual’s relationship reflection. In a consensual joint reading, positions for each participant can have meanings agreed with both. Do not use a position labelled “Them” to establish an absent person’s secret thoughts or future behaviour.

You may compare how a relationship looks from several perspectives, but label those as perspectives, not independently verified accounts. If a practical question can be clarified by an honest conversation with the other person, a reading should not replace that conversation with a claim of hidden knowledge.

Design a spread for an actual question

Suppose you are considering a new course of study. Begin with four positions: what draws me to it; what commitment it requires; what support I can investigate; what I need to clarify before deciding. Each has one function. Lay them in a square or a line if that helps you remember the sequence. No geometry grants the positions more authority than their definitions.

Test your design before drawing. Are two positions asking almost the same thing? Is a conclusion embedded in the wording? Does one position claim access to information the cards cannot establish? Would fewer cards address the question more clearly? Revise the layout now, while it is easy to separate design from interpretation.

Clarifiers and repeated draws

Before drawing a clarifier, write the exact ambiguity it will address. A clarifier can elaborate an interpretation, but it cannot independently verify it. If the issue is missing factual information, identify how to obtain that information instead. Repeatedly drawing because you dislike a result can replace reflection with reassurance-seeking.

For the exercise, allow at most one clarifier after a written explanation of its job. Then close the reading and record what remains unresolved. The purpose of that limit is to make your method observable, not to declare a universal law of tarot. You can evaluate the choice later in your practice record.

Check your reasoning

These recognition checks support practice. They do not certify interpretive skill.

When should position meanings be defined?

Defining positions first makes the interpretive procedure clearer.

Which relationship position fits this course’s boundaries?

The first concerns the querent’s experience rather than invented access to another mind.

What justifies an additional spread position?

More cards should serve the question, not the reader’s desired conclusion.

Retrieve before revealing

Why is “what is wrong with option B” a poor comparison with “why option A is right”?

The layout already assumes its conclusion. Compare both options through equivalent, open questions.

What can a clarifier do?

It can extend the symbolic interpretation of a defined ambiguity. It cannot independently prove the reading or supply missing factual evidence.

Practice and spaced review

  1. Choose a real reflective question and design three to five positions before drawing. Revise any overlap or leading wording.
  2. Read the same cards in two differently defined spreads and explain how the position changes the interpretation.
  3. If using a clarifier, record the exact ambiguity and what the extra card does not establish.

At your next session, recall one distinction from this lesson before opening it. Revisit it about a week later and again when a subsequent card study uses it. Mark uncertain material for review rather than treating a lookup as failure.

Read with the sources

Primary text: Waite, Part III §7: An Ancient Celtic Method of Divination. Compare the significator and positions 3–5 with this course’s explicitly labelled modern variant.

Practice workbook: Mary K. Greer, Tarot for Your Self. Work the journal and reading exercises in your edition. Preserve your original interpretations and record revisions separately.

Modern interpretive commentary: Rachel Pollack, Seventy-Eight Degrees of Wisdom. Read the matching card discussion after your own observation. Identify where Pollack’s psychological framing adds to or differs from Waite.
