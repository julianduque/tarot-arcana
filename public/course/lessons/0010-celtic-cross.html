The Celtic Cross: The Ten-Card Spread

By the end of this lesson You'll know the ten positions of the Celtic Cross — the most widely used spread in tarot — what each one asks, and how to read the whole layout as one story, bringing together card meanings, positions, and the elemental dignities from Lesson 9.

Why a bigger spread

Your three-card spread (Lesson 6) is perfect for a quick, focused question. The Celtic Cross is the workhorse for a fuller picture — past and future, inner and outer, hopes and likely outcome — in ten positions. It's the spread A. E. Waite laid out in the Pictorial Key, and it has been the standard ever since.1

#PositionWhat it asks
1The PresentThe heart of the matter, right now.
2The Challenge (crossing)What crosses or complicates it — laid sideways over card 1.
3The FoundationThe root or basis beneath the situation (deeper past, subconscious).
4The Recent PastWhat is just passing out of the situation.
5Above / GoalThe conscious aim, or best possible outcome, that crowns it.
6The Near FutureWhat is approaching next.
7Self / AdviceThe querent's own stance, or how to approach it.
8EnvironmentOutside influences — other people, circumstances.
9Hopes & FearsWhat the querent secretly wishes for, or dreads.
10The OutcomeWhere the whole thing tends, if the course holds.

Numbering and a few labels vary by author (some swap 3/5 as "below/above," or read 7 as "the querent"). Pick one convention and keep it — consistency matters more than which variant.

How to read it — don't just recite ten cards

The skill is connection, not ten mini-readings. A workable order:

  1. 1 + 2 first: the present crossed by its challenge — this is the whole question in miniature.
  2. The timeline: 4 → 1 → 6 (past, present, approaching) — the story's motion.
  3. The vertical axis: 3 (foundation, below) and 5 (goal, above) — what it rests on and what it reaches for.
  4. The staff, 7 → 10: you, your environment, your hopes/fears, and where it lands.
  5. Then dignities: look at neighbours' elements (Lesson 9). Is the Outcome well- or ill-dignified by what sits around it? Do fiery cards cluster? That texture is half the reading.

Watch position 9 (Hopes & Fears) — the same card can be a hope and a fear at once; that ambivalence is often the reading's most honest moment.

Everything you've learned lands here. Each of the ten cards you now read from structure — arcanum, suit/element, number or rank, decan, dignity. The Celtic Cross is not a new skill so much as the stage where all the previous ones perform together.

Retrieval practice

From memory: what do positions 1, 2, 5, and 10 represent?

1 the present (heart of the matter); 2 the challenge (crossing it); 5 above — the goal / best outcome; 10 the final outcome.

Which reading order turns ten cards into one story (not ten fragments)?

Read 1+2 together; then the timeline 4→1→6; then the vertical 3 (below) and 5 (above); then the staff 7→10; finally layer in elemental dignities between neighbours.

Practice before the next lesson

Homework

  1. Lay a full Celtic Cross for a real, open question about your own life. Set the ten cards in the positions above (card 2 crossing card 1).
  2. Read it in order, not left-to-right: 1+2, then 4→1→6, then 3 and 5, then the staff 7→10. Speak each card from its structure first.
  3. Add dignities. Note the elements of adjacent cards — is the Outcome (10) strengthened or weakened by its neighbours in the staff?
  4. Read the primary source: A. E. Waite's description of this spread in the Pictorial Key ("An Ancient Celtic Method of Divination") — compare his position names to the ones here.

Why read in a deliberate order: it forces you to build relationships between positions, which is exactly what separates a reading from a card-by-card recital.
