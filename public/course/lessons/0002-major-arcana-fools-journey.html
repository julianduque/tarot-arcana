The Major Arcana: The Fool's Journey

Lesson 2 of 22 · Major Arcana · Reading ~8 min · Practice separately

Use the Fool’s Journey as a memory structure, study the Fool closely, and recognize where narrative and historical attribution answer different questions.

This chapter includes full card studies. Read the core first, then study a few cards per sitting. RWS artwork, Golden Dawn correspondences and Thoth comparisons are explicitly labelled.

A story you can use without making it compulsory

The Fool’s Journey organizes the Major Arcana as a developmental narrative. We retain the familiar three rows of seven associated with Pollack’s approach: 1–7 concerns forming a self in the world; 8–14 examines an inward turn; 15–21 explores disruption, illumination and integration. Place the Fool outside the rows. This is an interpretive arrangement, not proof that a single author originally composed the trumps as that story.

A narrative helps memory because each image has neighbours. The danger is forcing every draw to reenact that narrative. The Emperor in a reading need not mean that you have reached a particular stage of maturity. The Moon need not mean you are regressing. A card’s place in a remembered sequence and its position in a particular spread are two different relationships.

Lay out the three acts

Act I moves from the Magician’s tools through Priestess, Empress, Emperor, Hierophant and Lovers to the Chariot’s directed movement. Treat that arrangement as questions about ability, receptivity, growth, authority, inherited teaching, commitment and direction. These themes can coexist in one adult life; they are not a test that children pass once and leave behind.

Act II moves through Strength, Hermit, Wheel, Justice, Hanged Man, Death and Temperance. Ask how determination becomes responsive strength; how solitude differs from withdrawal; what can be judged and what must be accepted; and how loss changes the conditions of a life. The arc is useful when it permits difficult distinctions. “All these cards mean change” discards most of what the images offer.

Act III brings Devil, Tower, Star, Moon, Sun, Judgement and World. Read the differences carefully: seeing an attachment, experiencing a rupture, finding orientation, living with uncertainty, seeing clearly, answering a call and completing an undertaking are not the same event. No sequence guarantees that a crisis will be followed by illumination on schedule.

Archetypes as an interpretive vocabulary

An archetypal reading notices recurring patterns such as the teacher, the traveller, the ruler or the encounter with an unfamiliar part of oneself. Jungian and post-Jungian tarot writers develop this vocabulary in different ways. Use it to ask a more exact question about the image and your response. Do not treat a person as an instance of a fixed universal category because a card suggests a mother, father or shadow figure.

A useful journal question is “What makes me recognize this as authority?” rather than “Which authority archetype controls me?” The first invites description of posture, setting and action. It also allows another person to see something different. Record personal associations as personal associations; a memory a picture evokes can matter without becoming part of the card’s historical meaning.

Numbers, titles and letters are separate fields

RWS uses VIII Strength and XI Justice. Thoth uses VIII Adjustment and XI Lust. These changes involve more than replacing a label, and the later card studies will compare their imagery and emphases. In the Golden Dawn correspondence framework, Strength is associated with Teth/Leo and Justice with Lamed/Libra. A printed numeral, Hebrew letter, astrological sign and path number must not be treated as four names for the same number.

The explorer makes those fields visible. The Tree always retains Golden Dawn path assignments. Selecting Thoth changes the comparison text and deck nomenclature, and explicitly displays the Emperor/Star letter distinction. It does not secretly turn the diagram into a different Tree.

The Fool deserves a full study

The Fool is not merely the introduction to the other twenty-one cards. Its image can invite attention to readiness, risk, openness, inexperience or a refusal to accept the usual calculation. Which interpretation fits depends on what you can point to and the question being asked. Study the following account with the physical card in front of you. Your first response may change once the dog, bundle, terrain and direction of attention are considered together.

Observe the RWS image

A young traveler stands very close to a rocky edge, with the face lifted toward the open sky. One hand holds a white rose; the other supports a staff and a small patterned bundle over the shoulder. A white dog rises beside the traveler's leg. The clothing is elaborate rather than practical, and a red feather projects from the cap. Pale mountains extend across the distance while a white sun occupies the upper corner of a yellow sky. Describe the suspended instant accurately: the picture does not show a completed leap. The contrast between upward attention and the ground immediately ahead gives the image its particular tension before any meaning is assigned.

RWS interpretation and application

Waite's Part II presents a traveler seeking experience, a much more expansive figure than the foolishness listed in Part III. Keep those two registers visible. A contemporary reading may suggest a first attempt, curiosity, willingness to be a beginner, or entering a situation without a settled identity. The same image can expose inadequate preparation or the wish to escape consequences. For a work question, trying a small prototype fits better than treating the card as permission to resign immediately. For a relationship question, openness needs communication and consent. The useful distinction is between an experiment with acknowledged uncertainty and a leap whose costs are being denied.

Golden Dawn framework and interpretive use

The Golden Dawn correspondence is Aleph, Air, path 11 between Kether and Chokmah. Aleph's conventional ox association can supply a mnemonic for raw generative energy, while Air suggests openness and movement before a fixed form develops. The placement near the summit of the Tree supports a meditation on possibility preceding a particular plan. These are interpretive associations inside a historical occult system; they do not explain why a shuffled card appeared. Avoid turning the Fool into a generic personality diagnosis. Ask which possibility is still unformed and what would allow it to meet actual experience.

Thoth — The Fool

Crowley treats zero as potential before differentiated existence, a religious-philosophical claim rather than historical proof about tarot's origin. Harris's green figure carries grapes and a flaming pine cone; a tiger, crocodile and circling forms turn Smith's spare journey into a burst of fertility and ecstasy. The change is substantial: RWS concentrates on a traveler at an uncertain edge, while Thoth gathers many forms of life around the initiating impulse. As a reading inference, ask whether a new idea needs exploratory space before it is forced into an existing plan. Keep that openness provisional. The image can encourage an unfamiliar experiment without declaring every intoxicating impulse wise or treating intensity as evidence of a revelation.

Distinguish a similar card

Compare the Fool with the Magician. Both can begin something, but the Fool has an unopened bundle and an uncertain destination; the Magician has tools displayed and a deliberate gesture. If a project needs permission to explore, the Fool may be a useful lens. If it needs a defined method and an accountable first action, the Magician asks the sharper question. Neither card proves readiness or incapacity.

Practice the story in both directions

Tell the three-act story once. Then choose a card and work backwards: name its picture, what distinguishes it from the cards beside it, and only then its role in your story. If the narrative makes every difficult card a necessary blessing, pause. Difficulty does not need to be spiritually justified to be read with care. If a story makes a card memorable but oversimplifies its range, keep the mnemonic and amend the interpretation.

Check your reasoning

These recognition checks support practice. They do not certify interpretive skill.

What does the three-act arrangement primarily provide?

Its value is interpretive and mnemonic, without requiring a universal history.

In Thoth, VIII is which card?

Thoth VIII is Adjustment; XI is Lust. RWS VIII is Strength.

Which comparison is most useful?

A distinction develops reading judgement; a generic similarity often conceals it.

Retrieve before revealing

Is the Fool’s Journey the only meaning of the trump sequence?

No. It is a useful modern interpretive framework. The cards also have particular histories, images and divinatory traditions that do not reduce to a single developmental plot.

What should a deck comparison preserve when Strength changes number?

Keep numeral, name, letter, astrology and diagram convention as separate fields. Do not infer that moving a numeral changes every other attribution.
