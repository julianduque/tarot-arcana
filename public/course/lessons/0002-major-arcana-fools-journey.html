The Major Arcana: The Fool's Journey

By the end of this lesson You'll read the 22 trumps not as 22 things to memorize but as a single story — the Fool's journey from 0 to 21 — and you'll be able to place any Major card into one of three clear acts. That story is the scaffold every individual trump will later hang on.

Why the Majors are "major"

In Lesson 1 you split the deck: 22 Major Arcana, 56 Minor. The Minors are the texture of daily life — this argument, that payment, this new idea. The Majors are the big archetypal forces moving underneath: initiation, love, death, fate, transcendence. When a reading is heavy with trumps, deep and non-trivial forces are in play, not just everyday weather.1

This is where tarot meets the psychology you care about: the trumps are close to what Jung called archetypes — universal patterns (the Mother, the Shadow, the Wise Old Man, rebirth) that recur across every culture's myths. Reading the Majors is learning to recognize which archetype is live in a situation.

The Fool is the traveller

The trumps are numbered 1–21, but the deck opens with a zero: The Fool. Zero is not "before one" — it's the traveller who moves through every other card. Read the 22 Majors in order and they tell one continuous story: the Fool sets out innocent (0), meets a sequence of teachers, trials, and revelations, and arrives whole (21). This reading is called the Fool's Journey, a phrase popularized by Eden Gray.2

One journey, three acts

Rachel Pollack's classic reading groups the 21 numbered trumps into three rows of seven — three acts of the same story.3 Learn the acts first; the individual cards get much easier once you know which act each lives in.

ActCardsThe realmThe work of this act
I1–7 · Magician → ChariotConsciousness — the outer worldBuilding an identity: finding your tools, teachers, loves, and will in society.
II8–14 · Strength → TemperanceThe unconscious — the inward turnGoing beneath the surface: solitude, letting go, endings, and inner balance.
III15–21 · Devil → WorldThe superconscious — the returnConfronting illusion and shock, then awakening to a larger, reintegrated whole.

A one-line walk through the story, so the shape is concrete: The Fool (0) sets out. He learns the tools of will and intuition (Magician, High Priestess), meets the mother and father of the world (Empress, Emperor), tradition and love (Hierophant, Lovers), and takes the reins (Chariot). He then turns inward — courage, solitude, and the turning of fate (Strength, Hermit, Wheel) — faces consequence, surrender, and death (Justice, Hanged Man, Death), and finds balance (Temperance). Finally he meets his own bondage and its shattering (Devil, Tower), is remade by hope and the great lights (Star, Moon, Sun), is reborn to a calling (Judgement), and dances complete (World).

Two numbering traps

Your decks will disagree about two things here — know them now:

Strength & Justice

Waite swapped them: in RWS, Strength = VIII and Justice = XI. Older Marseille decks (and Thoth) number them the other way. The images and meanings are unchanged — only the numbers move.

Thoth renames

Crowley renamed several trumps: Strength → Lust, Justice → Adjustment, Temperance → Art, Judgement → Aeon, The World → The Universe. Same positions in the journey.

Coming attractions. Every trump is also a door on the Tree of Life — each carries one Hebrew letter, one astrological sign or planet, and one path. The Fool is Aleph / Air; Death is Nun / Scorpio; The World is Tav / Saturn. You don't learn those yet — but they're waiting for you on the master correspondence sheet, and the Correspondences track will earn them card by card.

Retrieval practice

Without looking up: name the three acts of the Fool's Journey and the realm each explores.

  1. Act I (1–7): consciousness — building an identity in the outer world.
  2. Act II (8–14): the unconscious — the inward search and letting go.
  3. Act III (15–21): the superconscious — shock, awakening, and return to a larger whole.

From memory: what kind of force does a Major card signal, versus a Minor card?

A Major signals a big, archetypal, often fated force (initiation, death, transcendence); a Minor signals the everyday texture of a situation. Many trumps in a spread = deep forces at work.

Practice before the next lesson

Homework

  1. Lay the journey out. Pull all 22 Majors from a physical deck and lay them in three rows of seven — 1–7, 8–14, 15–21 — with the Fool (0) set above them all. Sit with the three rows as three chapters.
  2. Tell the story aloud. Walk 0 → 21, giving each card one sentence of the Fool's story in your own words. Stumbling is the point — it shows where the arc isn't yet yours.
  3. Two archetypes, honestly. Name the one trump you're most drawn to and the one you most resist. Write a sentence on each: which archetype is that, and why might it be live for you now? (This is the Jungian muscle the mission is after.)
  4. Read the primary source: skim Waite's Pictorial Key, Part III (the Greater Arcana), for his one-paragraph description of each trump — compare his words to the story you just told.

Why lay them out physically: seeing all 22 at once builds the mental map that turns 22 separate cards into one memorable arc.
